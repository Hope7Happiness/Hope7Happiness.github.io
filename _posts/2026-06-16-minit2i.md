<p>Training modern text-to-image (T2I) models often feels inaccessible, overshadowed by the perception that it requires massive infrastructure and highly complex engineering pipelines. We wanted to explore the opposite direction: how far can we go with a deliberately simple recipe and a manageable compute budget? The result is <strong>MiniT2I</strong>, a pixel-space diffusion model built on a straightforward architecture (<strong>MM-JiT</strong>) and minimal data design. Using an academic-sized model and computational resources on the order of standard ImageNet training, MiniT2I achieves highly competitive results on popular T2I benchmarks. Furthermore, this minimalist recipe remains stable as we increase model capacity. In this post, we share the story of what we did and the practical lessons we learned along the way. We are releasing our <a href="https://github.com/Hope7Happiness/t2i-release" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PyTorch</a> and <a href="https://github.com/PeppaKing8/minit2i-jax" target="_blank" rel="noopener">JAX</a> code, <a href="https://huggingface.co/MiniT2I" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Hugging Face</a> checkpoints, and a <a href="https://peppaking8.github.io/#/post/minit2i-l16-gallery" target="_blank" rel="noopener">gallery</a> of samples of our model.</p>

<h2 data-toc="Intro"><span class="hn">01</span>Isn't T2I too hard for us?</h2>
<p>On a broad spectrum of conditionality for image generation, class-conditional modeling on ImageNet has long been the default practice: controlled, cheap, and easy to benchmark with FID. However, real-life applications demand a shift toward the <em>rich-conditional side</em> of this spectrum. A classical example is text-to-image (T2I), where the condition transitions from labels to open-ended language. In T2I, the model must follow arbitrary prompts faithfully and deliver high visual quality, not just produce images that look realistic on average. Because the task is so much broader, succeeding at T2I is a much stronger signal that a method <em>genuinely scales</em> — and a more direct reflection of what we actually ask generative models to do.</p>
<figure class="figure--wide">
  <img src="/assets/images/posts/minit2i/imagegen-researchers-meme.png" alt="Meme contrasting the dream of generating magical images with the reality of inspecting ImageNet animal samples all day." loading="lazy" decoding="async">
</figure>
<p>Yet, shifting to T2I comes with an obvious catch: the nature of the problem makes it feel fundamentally more complicated and demanding than class-conditional generation. That perceived complexity spans everything from intricate model designs to massive compute budgets and heavily engineered data pipelines. Some giants like SD3, FLUX.1-dev, and DALL&middot;E 3, alongside unified generators (Show-o2, BAGEL, OpenUni, Tuna-2, etc.), produce beautiful images — but they charge an intimidating entry fee <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-sd3">Esser et al. 2024</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-flux">Black Forest Labs 2024</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-dalle3">Betker et al. 2023</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-showo2">Xie et al. 2025</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-bagel">Deng et al. 2025</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-openuni">S. Wu et al. 2025</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-tuna2">Liu et al. 2026</a>)</span>.</p>

<p>So it is natural (and quite ambitious) to ask: <em>Can we make text-to-image feel like ImageNet-level experimentation, with simple architecture, simple data, and a recipe short enough to hold in your head?</em></p>

  <p>We answer this question with a tentative "yes" — and the rest of this post explains how. If you take the text conditions produced by a pretrained language model and incorporate them as in-context conditioning, <em>text-to-image is actually not much different from class-conditional generation</em>. It is strikingly similar in terms of model architecture, the compute required, and even the volume of data the model needs to see.</p>
</div>

<p>This realization led us to <strong>MiniT2I</strong>: an intentionally plain, pixel-space flow-matching model. Using a simple MM-JiT backbone, readily available data, and an academic-scale compute budget, it reaches the performance frontier of pixel-space diffusion-based T2I systems.</p>
<p>In a nutshell, we were genuinely surprised by how far a minimalist, direct-pixel approach could take us. We hope MiniT2I can serve as a clean baseline for text-to-image generation: small enough to debug, strong enough to reveal real failures, and simple enough to reproduce, modify, and challenge.</p>
<p>The rest of this post replays the project step-by-step: we start by reproducing a standard baseline, drop it into pixel space, fix the sampler, delete the bloat, repair the data — and only then ask where we stand.</p>

<h2 data-toc="Preliminaries"><span class="hn">02</span>Two minutes of background</h2>
<p style="text-decoration: underline;"><em>Already fluent in diffusion, LDMs, and common T2I practice? <a data-sec="Setup" href="#s-2-setup">Skip ahead</a>.</em></p>
<p><strong>Flow matching.</strong> Flow matching is a diffusion-family algorithm for generative modeling <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-ddpm">Ho et al. 2020</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-flowmatch">Lipman et al. 2022</a>)</span>: learn how to transport pure noise into the data distribution. During training, we sample a noisy image on the straight path $z_t = t x + (1 - t)\epsilon$, where $x$ is a real image and $\epsilon$ is Gaussian noise. The model predicts the velocity $v = x - \epsilon$: the direction that walks the noisy sample back toward the real image. With a perfectly learned velocity field, gradually integrating those directions transports random noise into realistic images. For a more formal tutorial, the <a href="https://mlg.eng.cam.ac.uk/blog/2024/01/20/flow-matching.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cambridge MLG flow-matching introduction</a> is a good place to start.</p>
<p><strong>Latent vs. pixel space.</strong> High-resolution images are huge, so latent diffusion made the problem practical by compressing pixels into an autoencoder space, denoising there, and decoding back <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-ldm">Rombach et al. 2022</a>)</span>. This works beautifully and dominates much of modern T2I practice, but it comes with reconstruction artifacts and an extra encoder-decoder training stage. JiT showed a different route — stay in RGB, chop the image into large patches, and predict clean pixels so the denoiser survives the high-dimensional target <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-jit">Li and He 2025</a>)</span>.</p>
<p><strong>Architecture for T2I.</strong> For T2I, the job is to transfer information from the prompt into the generated image. The usual move is to extract prompt features with a pretrained text encoder, then let text and image tokens share attention while the image is denoised. SD3's MM-DiT is the modern reference: each modality gets its own stream weights, then everything mixes through joint attention <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-sd3">Esser et al. 2024</a>)</span>. We dissect its block structure in <a data-sec="MiniT2I" href="#s-3-minit2i">Section 04</a>. MM-DiT is conceptually simple, but it carries many small design choices worth questioning: which are necessary, and which are just inherited complexity?</p>

<p><strong>Classifier-free guidance.</strong> CFG was originally introduced for class-conditional diffusion, where the model is pushed toward a chosen class at inference time; in T2I, the same idea is extended from class labels to text prompts <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-cfg">Ho and Salimans 2022</a>)</span>. In formula form, $v_\mathrm{cfg} = v_u + s(v_c - v_u)$, where $v_c$ and $v_u$ are the predictions with and without the condition, and $s$ is the guidance scale. The unconditional branch $v_u$ is trained by randomly dropping the condition during training. ImageNet models often work with scales around 2-3; T2I needs stricter prompt following, so scales around 6 or higher are common.</p>

<h2 data-toc="Setup"><span class="hn">03</span>The minimum setup</h2>
<p>Before touching anything, we set one simple rule: <strong>remove every part that seems to be removable</strong>. For example, we use no image tokenizer, cascaded generation process, prompt rewriting, RL, or DPO stages. Next, we discuss our choice of data, text encoder, and evaluation benchmarks.</p>
<p><strong>Data.</strong> Standard practice today is to train on massive web-scale image-text datasets like LAION-5B or raw CC12M <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-laion400m">Schuhmann et al. 2021</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-laion5b">Schuhmann et al. 2022</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-cc12m">Changpinyo et al. 2021</a>)</span>. However, we found that their raw caption quality is intolerably low, which in practice severely slows down the training process. Instead, we rely on a publicly available VLM-recaptioned dataset: <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/CaptionEmporium/conceptual-captions-cc12m-llavanext" target="_blank" rel="noopener">LLaVA-recaptioned CC12M</a>.</p>
<p>Detailed recaptioning, however, comes with the cost of much longer sequences. After tokenization, the recaptioned prompts average about 100 tokens — up from less than 20 before recaptioning. We chose a 256-token text budget, which safely covers roughly 99% of the training prompts.</p>

<p><strong>Text encoder.</strong> For text, we freeze a FLAN-T5-Large encoder <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-flant5">Chung et al. 2022</a>)</span>. At just 341M parameters on the encoder side, it seems tiny and weak compared to the multi-billion-parameter encoders powering frontier T2I systems. However, we tried upgrading it — swapping in T5-XXL, Gemma2-2B, and Qwen3-VL-2B <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-gemma2">Gemma Team et al. 2024</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-qwen3vl">Bai et al. 2025</a>)</span> — but the larger models inflated the compute bill far more than they improved the numbers. So T5-L remained our compact yet effective choice.</p>
<p><strong>Evaluations.</strong> We report four common benchmarks, each probing a different slice of the problem:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>MSCOCO-30K FID</strong> measures distribution-level realism, but never asks whether the image followed the prompt <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-fid">Heusel et al. 2017</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-coco">Lin et al. 2014</a>)</span>.</li>
  <li><strong>GenEval</strong> checks object-level prompt following: existence, count, color, and position <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-geneval">Ghosh et al. 2023</a>)</span>.</li>
  <li><strong>DPG-Bench</strong> scores long-prompt alignment <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-dpg">Hu et al. 2024</a>)</span>.</li>
  <li><strong>PRISM-Bench</strong> is the broad stress test we save for the end: text rendering, style, imagination, and entities <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-prism">Fang et al. 2025</a>)</span>.</li>
</ul>

<h2 data-toc="MiniT2I"><span class="hn">04</span>The path toward MiniT2I</h2>
<h3>A strong pixel-space MM-DiT baseline</h3>
<p>Our first pixel model was the most straightforward thing we could build: an SD3-style MM-DiT rescaled to our B/32 ablation budget, under 300M trainable generator parameters, with latents swapped for raw pixels <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-sd3">Esser et al. 2024</a>)</span>. At 512×512 with 32×32 patches, the image becomes 256 tokens; add 256 text tokens and the whole sequence is 512 tokens, comfortably in the range of ImageNet-scale experiments.</p>

<p>Dropping the codec is an immediate win on compute. The latent B/32 forward costs about <strong class="cost-high">1117 VAE GFLOPs + 262 backbone GFLOPs = 1379 GFLOPs</strong> per image; in pixels the codec is gone and the step falls to about <strong class="cost-low">265 GFLOPs</strong>. To fit the pixel-space representation, we simply use JiT's timestep sampler and noise schedule.</p>

<p>With this change, the pixel model matches the latent baseline on FID at a fraction of the per-step cost. This was our first useful sign that pixel space itself is not the bottleneck: given the right calibration, it learns about as well as latent space.</p>

<p>A design choice inherited from <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-jit">Li and He 2025</a>)</span> turns out to be essential: for high-dimensional pixel-space diffusion, the network should directly predict the clean image, i.e., <strong>x-prediction</strong>. In high-dimensional pixel space, the loss space and the network's direct output space are separate choices: each can be written in clean-image x, noise &#x3F5;, or velocity v coordinates. This gives nine valid combinations. Holding the B/32 recipe fixed, x-prediction is stable across loss spaces, while direct &#x3F5;- or v-prediction collapses. We therefore use x-prediction with a v-loss throughout MiniT2I.</p>

<h3>MM-JiT: A simple multimodal architecture</h3>
<p>With a solid pixel baseline in hand, the next question was where to spend parameters. Starting from the MM-DiT baseline, we made two main architectural changes.</p>

<p><strong>Text adapter blocks.</strong> We first add two small text-only Transformer adapter blocks before joint attention. The frozen T5 features are useful, but they were not trained to be consumed by a pixel denoiser, so these adapters give the model a cheap place to reshape the text tokens before they meet image tokens.</p>
<p><strong>Removing AdaLN conditioning.</strong> SD3-style MM-DiT conditions on the prompt twice: first through joint attention between image and text tokens, and again through an AdaLN branch that turns pooled text and timestep embeddings into per-block scale, shift, and gate parameters. We found the second route unnecessary for a compact model. MM-JiT keeps the double-stream joint attention, but removes the per-block AdaLN branch; image and text tokens still use modality-specific normalizations and MLPs, and the noise level remains visible from the corrupted image input. This brings the backbone closer to a plain pre-norm Transformer, a design pattern widely used in recent generative Transformer models <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-imf">Geng et al. 2025b</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-pixelmeanflow">Lu et al. 2026</a>; <a class="cite-link" href="#ref-i1">Zeng et al. 2026</a>)</span>.</p>

<p><strong>We fine-tune on this mixture for 40K steps</strong> — pretty short compared to the 250K-step pretraining run, but against a 120K-image pool it is a heavy exposure to that high-quality distribution.</p>
<p>The shape should feel familiar from LLM training: pretraining buys coverage, supervised fine-tuning teaches the model what a good answer looks like — same objective, different data, different behavior <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-instructgpt">Ouyang et al. 2022</a>)</span>. The ablation below confirms the division of labor; neither pool can do both jobs. Fine-tuning on more CC12M does nothing for alignment, pretraining on the alignment mix alone collapses coverage, and mixing the two dilutes both.</p>

<h2 data-toc="Scaling"><span class="hn">05</span>What happens when we scale up?</h2>
<p>Every decision so far was made at B/32, our ablation workhorse with its tidy 256 image tokens. The natural next question is whether the same recipe survives a larger setting. B/16 keeps roughly the same parameter count but switches to 16×16 patches, which quadruples the tokens to 1024; L/16 holds that sequence length and grows the generator to 912M parameters. For details, please refer to our <a href="https://github.com/Hope7Happiness/t2i-release" target="_blank" rel="noopener">code release</a>.</p>
<p>The answer is reassuring: the unchanged recipe trains stably at both settings, and the numbers come out stronger than the B/32 ablation.</p>

<h2 data-toc="Extensions"><span class="hn">07</span>Two extensions: LoRA and few-step distillation</h2>
<p>A baseline earns its keep when ordinary follow-up tricks still work on it, so we tried two: downstream adaptation via LoRA, and few-step sampling via Mean Flow.</p>
<p><strong>LoRA.</strong> The worry with a small model is that it may not contain enough world knowledge to adapt cleanly. So we tried, and the results are surprisingly good. We fine-tune MiniT2I-B/16 on two ~1K-image captioned datasets, <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/lambdalabs/naruto-blip-captions" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Naruto BLIP captions</a> and <a href="https://huggingface.co/datasets/lambdalabs/pokemon-blip-captions" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Pokemon BLIP captions</a> <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-lora">Hu et al. 2021</a>)</span>, attaching adapters to the attention, MLP, and text projection layers and training only the adapters for 400 steps at batch size 16. The style transfers and the prompts keep working.</p>

<p><strong>Mean Flow distillation.</strong> The default 100-step Euler sampler is fine for research, but it is painful for real interaction. To fix the speed bottleneck, we turned to Mean Flow, a popular distillation method that learns an average velocity field for few-step generation <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-meanflow">Geng et al. 2025a</a>)</span>. Initializing the student with the teacher's weights, we followed the pixel Mean Flow approach <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-pixelmeanflow">Lu et al. 2026</a>)</span> to distill MiniT2I-B/16 into a 4-step pixel-space model. The process took just a 50K-step run — a fraction of the teacher's training cost. After distillation, throughput improved by ~50×, while visual quality and benchmark scores held remarkably steady.</p>
<p>The most surprising takeaway was that the distilled quality is largely agnostic to the dataset. When we ran the distillation using our standard pretraining dataset, it achieved approximately the same visual quality but yielded even higher diversity in the generated images.</p>

<h2 data-toc="Discussion"><span class="hn">08</span>Discussion</h2>

<h3>Is pixel-space diffusion promising?</h3>
<p>Cautiously, yes. Removing the codec makes the model conceptually clean and puts the raw <strong>dimensionality</strong> of images directly in play — both the opportunity and the cost of pixel space. At 512×512, we use 16×16 patches for a manageable 1024 image tokens, and the same architecture can be applied at 1024×1024 by simply doubling the patch size. But pushing much further, to 4K and beyond, will still require real engineering: either much longer sequences, larger and harder-to-denoise patches, or more efficient attention. The advantage is not that high resolution becomes free, but that the bottleneck is exposed inside the generator itself, with no separate codec ceiling in the way.</p>

<p>The real challenge is <strong>guidance</strong>. T2I needs a large CFG scale (around 6 is common practice for both SD3 and our model), and CFG is ultimately a brute-force trick: a linear extrapolation away from the model's own predictions, which can push individual tokens off the data manifold in inconsistent directions. A latent system can hide some of that behind the decoder; in pixel space, the artifacts land directly in the output (as shown in the following figure).</p>

<p>Nevertheless, we believe pixel-space diffusion remains a promising direction, for two reasons. First, it can be <strong>truly lossless</strong>. A latent generator is upper-bounded by the reconstruction quality of its VAE, and as denoisers grow stronger, that ceiling may become a real bottleneck, especially for image editing, where fine details must survive a round trip through the codec. Second, it collapses training into <strong>one stage</strong>. The standard practice is two: first train a VAE, then train a denoiser inside its latent space, even though the two stages optimize mismatched objectives; a VAE trained for reconstruction is not necessarily a good space to diffuse in <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-diffusability">Skorokhodov et al. 2025</a>)</span>. The bet may also extend beyond research <span class="citation">(<a class="cite-link" href="#ref-reve">Reve 2026</a>)</span>.</p>

<h3>Please try MiniT2I!</h3>
<p>If this project changed our minds about anything, it is this: text-to-image is now both powerful and accessible. For the compute cost of a few ImageNet runs, we can work on a task that actually reflects the problems industry systems are built around. <em>The broader the task, the harder it is for an idea to survive</em> — and that is exactly why we prefer T2I over class-conditional generation. Most methods that buy a marginal ImageNet FID improvement were never going to transfer to broader vision tasks anyway. T2I catches those fragile ideas early.</p>
<p>The point we most want to land is this: we do not have to — and arguably cannot — "earn" our way to T2I by first perfecting ImageNet FID. And diving into T2I no longer means a painful stretch of trial and error. The baseline is public, and the recipe is short. We hope this work makes T2I feel less like a closed industrial artifact and encourages more research effort to run real experiments on it.</p>
<p>Looking ahead, the biggest unsolved problem is still data. Better alignment data and benchmarks that don't just reward a narrow synthetic aesthetic will do far more for the field than adding another layer of mystery machinery. Beyond that, there are natural extensions for this minimalist approach: direct-pixel image editing without the codec round trip, unified generation/understanding models, and scaling up to video or 3D. Those frontiers will be harder because the data gets noisier and compute requirements rise. But the core message remains: T2I is no longer a walled garden you can only cite from the outside. Please try it, break it, and make the next baseline even simpler!</p>
